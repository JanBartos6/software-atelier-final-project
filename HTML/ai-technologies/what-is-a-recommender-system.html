What is a recommender system, and how does it work?

Every time you open Netflix, Spotify or TikTok, something has already decided what you will see first.

That “something” is a recommender system. It is probably the AI we meet most often without noticing it, and it is the clearest example of an AI that interacts not with the physical world or with our bodies, but with our minds.

by Minh Quan Tran

Artificial intelligence can be defined as the design of agents that perceive their environment and act to achieve goals. Its everyday applications can be grouped by what they interact with:

  • The world: autonomous vehicles and robots.
  • The human voice: voice assistants and machine translation.
  • The human body: medical imaging and wearable health monitors.
  • The human psyche: systems that model our preferences, our attention and our emotions.

The most prominent example of the last group is the recommender system: software that suggests items likely to interest a particular user, such as films, songs, products or posts.

Why do we need it? Because of information overload. Today’s catalogues contain far more options than anyone could ever look through. Nobody can browse every film on a streaming service or every video uploaded in a day, so a system has to choose for us which few items deserve a place on our screen.

What a recommender learns from

To make that choice, a recommender learns from two kinds of signals:

  • Explicit feedback: things we tell the system on purpose, like ratings, likes or a click on “not interested”.
  • Implicit feedback: things the system infers from our behavior, such as clicks, watch time, skips and searches. This is by far the most important source, because we produce it constantly and without thinking.

TikTok, for example, states that its For You feed is based on user interactions (the videos you like, share or watch to the end), on information about the videos themselves (captions, sounds, hashtags) and, with a lower weight, on device and account settings such as language and country.

a recommender doesn’t need to ask what you like: it watches what you do.

One word of caution before going further: many of the numbers about recommender systems come from the companies that build them. In these articles, figures that companies report about their own products are marked as such, because they cannot be independently verified.

How a recommender chooses

There are two basic ideas: recommend things that look like what you already liked, or recommend what people similar to you liked. The classic approaches are three:

  • Content-based filtering: it recommends items similar to those you liked. If you watched three space documentaries, it suggests a fourth one.
  • Collaborative filtering: it recommends what similar users liked. If people with tastes like yours loved a certain album, you will probably like it too, even if it has nothing in common with what you have heard so far. Amazon made this idea famous with its “customers who bought this item also bought” suggestions.
  • Matrix factorization: it represents every user and every item as a list of numbers, a vector of “hidden factors” learned from the data. Nobody tells the system what these factors mean, but one might end up measuring how much a film is a comedy, another how much it is aimed at children. A user and an item that point in the same direction are a good match.

Large platforms combine these methods with deep learning. YouTube is a good example, because it has published how its system works. It uses two stages:

  1. Candidate generation: a first neural network reduces millions of videos to a few hundred that might interest you.
  2. Ranking: a second network scores those candidates in much more detail and orders the few dozen that are actually shown.

An important detail is what the ranking model tries to predict. YouTube’s model doesn’t predict whether you will click on a video, but how long you are expected to watch it. This choice was made because clicks can be fooled by misleading thumbnails, while watch time is harder to fake. It also shows something that is true of every recommender:

every recommender optimizes a goal that someone has chosen.

Clicks, watch time, purchases, likes: each goal produces a different feed, and none of them is neutral.

The feedback loop

Finally, recommenders work in a feedback loop. You react to what you are shown, and your reactions become new training data for the next round of recommendations. This makes personalization very fast: after a few minutes on a new app, the feed already seems to know you. But the loop also reinforces existing patterns, because the system mostly learns from what it decided to show you in the first place.

A system that learns this quickly what keeps us watching is extremely powerful. But how much of what we watch is really chosen by the algorithm, and why do we find it so hard to put the phone down?
