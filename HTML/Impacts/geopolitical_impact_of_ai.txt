THE GEOPOLITICAL IMPACT OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE How AI is reshaping
global power, security and international relations

INTRODUCTION — AI AS A NEW SOURCE OF GEOPOLITICAL POWER

Artificial intelligence (AI) is no longer simply a technological
innovation used to make everyday tasks faster. It is becoming a
strategic resource that can influence economic growth, military
capabilities, political stability and the international balance of
power. From analysing satellite images to developing medicines, managing
financial systems and generating information, AI is changing the way
governments and societies operate.

This transformation has important geopolitical consequences. Countries
that develop advanced AI systems, manufacture the hardware needed to run
them and attract highly skilled researchers may gain significant
economic and strategic advantages. At the same time, countries that
depend on foreign technology risk becoming vulnerable to decisions made
outside their borders.

AI is therefore increasingly connected to national security and
international competition. Governments are investing in research,
computing infrastructure, semiconductor production and education. They
are also debating how AI should be regulated and how to prevent its
misuse. These choices are not only about technology: they concern
sovereignty, influence and the distribution of power in the world.

Unlike traditional resources such as oil or natural gas, AI depends on a
complex combination of data, computer chips, energy, software,
scientific knowledge and skilled workers. These resources are unevenly
distributed. As a result, the development of AI may reinforce the
influence of countries that already possess strong technological and
economic foundations, while creating new opportunities for others.

This article examines four major dimensions of AI’s geopolitical impact:
the technological competition between major powers, its consequences for
military and international security, the challenges of global
governance, and the possible future of the international order.

PAGE 1 — THE GLOBAL AI RACE: UNITED STATES, CHINA AND EUROPE

The competition between the United States and China is one of the
clearest examples of AI becoming a geopolitical issue. Both countries
consider advanced technology essential to their long-term economic
strength and national security. AI is central to this competition
because it can improve productivity, support scientific research,
strengthen military systems and influence the global technology market.

The United States benefits from a powerful technology sector, major
research universities, access to investment capital and companies that
develop advanced AI models and computing systems. American firms play an
important role in designing software, developing AI services and
supplying the chips and cloud infrastructure required by many
organisations around the world. This gives the United States
considerable influence over the technologies used internationally.

China has also made AI a national priority. Its large domestic market,
substantial industrial capacity, expanding research community and
government support provide important advantages. AI is being developed
for applications ranging from manufacturing and logistics to healthcare,
public services and security. China also seeks to reduce its dependence
on foreign technology, particularly in strategically important areas
such as advanced semiconductors.

The rivalry is not limited to creating the most powerful AI model. It
also concerns control over the supply chain. Advanced semiconductors are
particularly important because they provide much of the computing power
required to train and operate sophisticated AI systems. Their production
depends on specialised equipment, materials, intellectual property and
highly complex manufacturing processes. Only a relatively small number
of countries and companies possess the capabilities needed to produce
the most advanced chips.

This concentration creates geopolitical leverage. Export restrictions,
trade disputes or disruptions to manufacturing can affect another
country’s ability to develop advanced technology. Governments may
therefore use trade policy and investment controls to protect their
strategic interests. Such measures can slow the transfer of sensitive
technologies, but they can also increase tensions and encourage
competing technological ecosystems.

Europe occupies a different position in this competition. It has strong
scientific institutions, industrial expertise and an important role in
the global semiconductor equipment supply chain. However, it has
generally struggled to match the scale of American private investment
and the size of some Chinese technology programmes. European
policymakers have consequently placed considerable emphasis on research,
industrial capacity, digital sovereignty and regulation.

The European Union’s approach is distinctive because it seeks to
encourage innovation while establishing rules for the use of AI. Its AI
Act introduces a risk-based regulatory framework, with stricter
requirements for certain high-risk applications and restrictions on some
unacceptable uses. This approach may strengthen public trust and
establish international standards, although businesses must also manage
the costs and complexity of compliance.

The wider lesson is that the AI race is not a simple contest with one
winner. Leadership can come from different areas: designing chips,
building data centres, developing models, supplying industrial
equipment, attracting researchers or setting regulatory standards.
Countries that control several parts of this ecosystem are likely to
have greater strategic independence.

PAGE 2 — AI, MILITARY POWER AND INTERNATIONAL SECURITY

Artificial intelligence is changing the nature of military power. Armed
forces have long used computers and automated systems, but recent
advances in machine learning and data analysis create new possibilities.
AI can process large quantities of information, identify patterns,
support decision-making and help military organisations respond more
quickly to complex situations.

One important application is intelligence and surveillance. Military
organisations collect information from satellites, drones, radar
systems, communications and other sources. AI can help analyse these
large datasets, detect unusual activity and identify potential threats.
This may allow commanders to obtain a clearer picture of events,
although the quality of the result depends on the accuracy of the data
and the reliability of the system.

AI can also support logistics and planning. Predicting equipment
failures, managing supply chains and allocating resources are essential
parts of military operations. Better predictions may reduce costs and
improve readiness. In addition, AI-assisted systems can help analyse
possible scenarios and provide recommendations. However, military
decisions involve uncertainty, ethical judgement and political
consequences. Human responsibility remains essential.

Autonomous weapons are among the most controversial applications. These
systems can select or engage targets with varying degrees of human
involvement. Some technologies already automate parts of defensive or
targeting processes, while more advanced autonomous capabilities raise
difficult questions about accountability and control.

The central concern is whether a machine should be allowed to make
decisions that can directly result in the use of lethal force. If an
autonomous system mistakenly identifies a civilian as a threat,
responsibility may be difficult to establish. The problem becomes even
more serious if several automated systems interact during a rapidly
developing conflict. A technical error or misunderstanding could
escalate a situation before human decision-makers have time to
intervene.

AI is also changing cybersecurity. It can help defenders detect
suspicious behaviour, identify vulnerabilities and respond to attacks.
At the same time, malicious actors may use AI to automate parts of
cyberattacks, create convincing fraudulent messages or search for
weaknesses in digital systems. This creates a continuous competition
between those seeking to protect networks and those attempting to
compromise them.

Another major security challenge is disinformation. Generative AI can
produce realistic text, images, audio and video. These tools can be used
to create fake political statements, imitate public figures or generate
misleading content during elections and international crises. The
ability to produce persuasive material quickly and at low cost may make
information manipulation easier to scale.

The danger is not limited to convincing people that a particular false
story is true. A constant flow of fabricated material can also weaken
public confidence in authentic information. Citizens may become less
able to distinguish reliable reporting from manipulation, while
governments may struggle to establish what happened during a crisis.
This can damage democratic institutions and complicate diplomacy.

AI does not automatically make conflict more likely, nor does it
guarantee military superiority. Its effects depend on the systems being
developed, the people operating them and the political decisions
surrounding their use. Nevertheless, countries that integrate AI
effectively into defence, intelligence and cybersecurity may gain
important advantages. This creates pressure on other states to invest as
well, potentially contributing to a new technological arms race.

PAGE 3 — ECONOMIC POWER, DIGITAL DEPENDENCE AND GLOBAL INEQUALITY

The geopolitical impact of AI extends far beyond military competition.
AI may transform productivity and economic organisation, influencing
which countries attract investment, create high-value industries and
shape global trade.

Businesses can use AI to automate repetitive tasks, improve forecasts,
analyse markets and develop new products. In manufacturing, AI systems
can help monitor production and predict equipment failures. In
healthcare, they can support medical research and assist professionals
in analysing information. In agriculture, they can help monitor crops
and manage resources. These applications may increase efficiency and
create new economic opportunities.

However, the benefits will not necessarily be distributed equally.
Countries with reliable electricity, high-speed networks, advanced
research institutions, access to computing power and skilled workers are
better positioned to adopt AI. Wealthier economies may therefore benefit
earlier and more extensively. Countries with weaker infrastructure could
find it difficult to compete, even if their populations have the talent
and ambition to participate.

This raises the possibility of a new digital divide. In the past,
international inequality was often discussed in terms of access to
industrial infrastructure, education and financial resources. In the AI
era, access to computing capacity, high-quality data and advanced
digital services may become equally important. If a small number of
countries control the infrastructure on which others depend, existing
economic inequalities could become more difficult to overcome.

Dependence on foreign technology is also a question of sovereignty. Many
governments and businesses rely on international providers for cloud
computing, software, data storage and AI services. These services can be
efficient and affordable, but reliance on a limited number of suppliers
creates risks. Changes in prices, access policies, export restrictions
or international sanctions may affect a country’s ability to use
essential digital infrastructure.

For this reason, governments increasingly discuss technological
sovereignty: the ability to make independent decisions about critical
technologies and maintain access to essential systems. Complete
technological independence is unrealistic for most countries because
modern supply chains are international. A more practical objective is to
reduce excessive dependence, diversify suppliers, develop local
expertise and establish reliable partnerships.

AI may also change the relationship between governments and
multinational corporations. Some private companies control enormous
computing resources, advanced models and global digital platforms. Their
decisions can influence access to information, business opportunities
and technological development across national borders. Governments must
therefore consider how to regulate powerful companies while preserving
innovation and avoiding excessive concentration of power.

For developing countries, AI presents both risks and opportunities. It
may help improve education, public administration, agriculture and
healthcare without requiring every country to build an entire technology
industry from the beginning. Local researchers and entrepreneurs can
adapt existing tools to regional languages and needs. Yet these
opportunities depend on access, affordability, appropriate training and
the ability to protect local data and interests.

The geopolitical question is consequently not only which country
develops the most advanced AI. It is also which countries can use AI
productively, participate in its development and benefit from the value
it creates. International cooperation, education and investment in
digital infrastructure will be important in preventing AI from widening
the gap between wealthy and poorer societies.

PAGE 4 — GLOBAL GOVERNANCE AND THE FUTURE OF INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS

AI develops across national borders, but laws and political institutions
remain largely organised at the national level. This creates a major
governance challenge. A system developed in one country may be used by
businesses and individuals around the world. Its effects can cross
borders, while responsibility for its design, deployment and misuse may
be spread across several organisations and jurisdictions.

Governments are responding in different ways. Some prioritise innovation
and private-sector development. Others place greater emphasis on state
supervision, security or social control. The European Union has pursued
a regulatory approach based on the level of risk associated with
different AI applications. International organisations and diplomatic
forums have also encouraged discussions about safety, human rights,
transparency and responsible development.

However, reaching international agreement is difficult. Countries do not
necessarily share the same political values, economic interests or
security priorities. A government may see a technology as an opportunity
for economic growth, while another may consider the same technology a
security threat. States may also hesitate to accept rules that they
believe could weaken their strategic position.

Despite these differences, certain principles could form the basis of
cooperation. Governments can work towards greater transparency in
high-impact AI systems, clearer responsibility when systems cause harm,
stronger cybersecurity practices and safeguards against discrimination.
International dialogue may also help establish expectations concerning
autonomous weapons and the use of AI during armed conflict.

Cooperation is particularly important because AI-related risks are not
restricted to individual countries. Cyberattacks can affect
international infrastructure, disinformation can spread across borders,
and failures in widely used systems can have consequences for many
economies. Scientific cooperation can also help researchers identify
technical risks and develop more reliable systems.

At the same time, international governance must avoid creating rules so
restrictive that they prevent smaller countries and organisations from
participating in technological development. If regulation is designed
only by the most powerful states and companies, it may reinforce
existing inequalities. Developing countries need a meaningful role in
discussions about standards, access, education and the distribution of
benefits.

The future geopolitical order will probably be shaped by several
competing trends. First, technological competition is likely to continue
as countries seek greater control over strategic infrastructure. Second,
economic interdependence will remain important because no country can
easily produce every component of the AI supply chain independently.
Third, international institutions will face increasing pressure to
develop rules that keep pace with technological change.

AI could therefore produce two very different outcomes. In one scenario,
competition becomes increasingly intense. Countries restrict access to
technology, build separate digital ecosystems and treat AI primarily as
an instrument of national power. This could deepen mistrust, increase
inequality and make international cooperation more difficult.

In another scenario, governments recognise that shared challenges
require common solutions. They maintain strategic competition but
cooperate on safety standards, scientific research, cybersecurity and
the prevention of serious abuses. Such cooperation would not eliminate
geopolitical rivalry, but it could reduce the risk that technological
competition becomes destabilising.

CONCLUSION — AI WILL HELP DEFINE THE NEXT BALANCE OF POWER

Artificial intelligence is becoming one of the most important
technologies shaping international relations. It influences economic
competitiveness, military capabilities, political communication and the
independence of states. As a result, the ability to develop and use AI
is increasingly connected to national power.

The competition between the United States and China demonstrates how
technological leadership has become a strategic priority. Europe’s
regulatory approach shows that influence can also come from establishing
standards and shaping how technologies are used. Meanwhile, the military
applications of AI raise urgent questions about human control,
accountability and international security.

Yet the most important issue may be how the benefits and risks of AI are
distributed. If access to computing power, expertise and infrastructure
remains concentrated in a small number of countries and companies, AI
could reinforce existing inequalities. If access becomes broader and
international cooperation improves, the technology could also support
economic development and help address shared problems.

Ultimately, AI is not simply a tool that countries use; it is a
technology that may reshape the relationships between them. The future
balance of power will depend not only on who builds the most advanced
systems, but also on who controls the infrastructure, establishes the
rules and ensures that technological progress serves society. The
challenge for governments is to pursue innovation and national security
without sacrificing international stability, human rights and
cooperation.
