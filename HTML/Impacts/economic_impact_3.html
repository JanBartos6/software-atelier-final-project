Adaptation

Part 3 of 4: jobs that change instead of disappearing

Pressure is not the same as replacement. This page looks at a company that found the limits of automation, and at jobs where AI takes some tasks and the job survives.

by Changlong Ma

When replacement goes too far

Replacement can be overdone. In early 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million chats in its first month, two-thirds of customer service, work equal to 700 agents. In May 2025 its CEO told Bloomberg that cost had been “a too predominant evaluation factor” and had led to “lower quality”. Klarna went back to recruiting human agents so customers can always reach a person, while the chatbot still takes about two-thirds of inquiries. The cheap, routine part got automated. The hard part came back to people.

Jobs that change instead of disappearing

Radiology is the textbook forecast that went wrong. In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton said people should stop training radiologists because AI would beat them within five to ten years. According to Fortune, the number of active US radiologists has since grown about 10%, the average salary reached $571,000 in 2025, and caseloads rose 25% between 2018 and early 2025. Hinton has since said he meant image analysis only. Reading a scan is one task inside a job that also includes consulting other doctors, monitoring patients and performing procedures. Cheaper imaging also produced more scans, so there was more to read.

The pattern is common. A job is a bundle of tasks, and AI usually takes some of them. If the freed time goes to something people will pay for, or if cheaper output raises demand, the job survives and shifts. If the bundle was mostly the automated task and demand is fixed, the job shrinks. The World Economic Forum (WEF) growth list reflects the first case: nurses, secondary school teachers, and frontline work such as delivery and construction, all roles where a person’s presence or accountability is part of the product.

What changes for the person in the role is the skill mix. If the support-agent study from the previous page generalises, the tool raises the floor, so judgement, handling a difficult customer and knowing when the tool is wrong become what separates workers. The WEF finds that 77% of employers plan to retrain staff, while 63% name skills gaps as their main barrier.

There is a human side to this as well. In the customer support study, the AI assistant also improved customer sentiment and made agents more likely to stay in the job. That is the optimistic version of the story: a tool that makes a hard job less stressful as well as faster. It is not guaranteed. The same tool could just as easily be used to set higher targets for the same number of people, and the data so far does not tell us which will win.
