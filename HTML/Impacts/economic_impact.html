Economic Impact

AI in everyday life: who adapts, who gets replaced, what comes next

Most people met artificial intelligence this way: a phone suggested the end of a sentence, a map rerouted around a crash, a streaming app guessed the next film. Nobody called it a revolution, and that is the point. AI is not arriving as one dramatic event. It is seeping into ordinary tools, one feature at a time, and the economic effects are building the same quiet way.

This article asks three practical questions. Where does AI already touch daily life, and what is it doing to the economy? Which jobs are under real pressure, and which are changing rather than disappearing? And what new kinds of work are appearing? The evidence so far is more mixed than either the optimists or the doomsayers admit, so where the data is thin, I say so.

by Changlong Ma

Where it already is

Half of American adults now use an AI chatbot, up from a third in 2024, according to a Pew survey from February 2026. Pew notes the 2024 question was worded slightly differently, so treat the jump as rough. And that number only counts people who type to a chatbot on purpose. It misses the AI nobody opens: the filter that stops a fraudulent card payment, the spam folder, the map that routes around a crash, the app that transcribes a lecture.

For most people the daily effect is small: a few minutes saved on a draft, a translation good enough to read an email in a language you do not speak, a first explanation of a topic that you then check. For a company the same small saving repeats thousands of times a day. That is where economics starts.

What it does to the economy

The honest summary is that the money is arriving faster than the proof. The IMF estimates that AI-related investment added about half a percentage point to US GDP growth in 2025, and cites outside estimates that global spending could top $2 trillion in 2026. Much of that is data centres, chips and power, not software people use. The same IMF section warns that the payoff from debt-financed AI investment “could prove illusory”, and that a bust could mean sharp market falls and layoffs.

On the worker side, the best evidence is a study of 5,179 customer support agents by Erik Brynjolfsson and co-authors. With an AI assistant suggesting replies, agents resolved about 14% more issues per hour. The gain was 34% for the least experienced agents and minimal for the most experienced. In that case AI worked as an equaliser: it copied the habits of top performers and handed them to newcomers.

Scaled up, Goldman Sachs Research estimates that generative AI could raise labour productivity in developed economies by about 15% once fully adopted. Its baseline has 6 to 7% of US workers displaced (range 3 to 14%) and unemployment up by roughly half a point during the transition. Those are projections from 2025, not measurements. And productivity gains do not automatically become wages. Who keeps them, the firm, the customer or the worker, depends on bargaining power. The IMF’s own research finds that middle-skilled workers in highly exposed jobs are the ones being left out.

Jobs under pressure

The pressure is real, but it is landing in a specific place: the first rung of the ladder. A Stanford study led by Brynjolfsson, using ADP payroll data on millions of US workers through June 2026, finds that employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in the same fields show no comparable gap, and the authors find no sign of broad job losses across the economy. Firms are not firing people so much as hiring fewer juniors. The authors call the results early and descriptive, not proof of cause, and the gap has widened with each update since 2025.

That fits the World Economic Forum’s 2025 employer survey, which lists cashiers and administrative assistants among the fastest-declining roles, now joined by graphic designers, and finds that 41% of employers plan to cut staff where AI can automate tasks. The exposed work is the routine kind: data entry, scripted customer replies, first-draft design, basic code.

Replacement can also be overdone. In early 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million chats in its first month, two-thirds of customer service, work equal to 700 agents. In May 2025 its CEO told Bloomberg that cost had been “a too predominant evaluation factor” and had led to “lower quality”. Klarna went back to recruiting human agents so customers can always reach a person, while the chatbot still takes about two-thirds of inquiries. The cheap, routine part got automated. The hard part came back to people.

Jobs that change instead of disappearing

Radiology is the textbook forecast that went wrong. In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton said people should stop training radiologists because AI would beat them within five to ten years. According to Fortune, the number of active US radiologists has since grown about 10%, the average salary reached $571,000 in 2025, and caseloads rose 25% between 2018 and early 2025. Hinton has since said he meant image analysis only. Reading a scan is one task inside a job that also includes consulting other doctors, monitoring patients and performing procedures. Cheaper imaging also produced more scans, so there was more to read.

The pattern is common. A job is a bundle of tasks, and AI usually takes some of them. If the freed time goes to something people will pay for, or if cheaper output raises demand, the job survives and shifts. If the bundle was mostly the automated task and demand is fixed, the job shrinks. The WEF’s growth list reflects the first case: nurses, secondary school teachers, and frontline work such as delivery and construction, all roles where a person’s presence or accountability is part of the product.

What changes for the person in the role is the skill mix. If the support-agent study generalises, the tool raises the floor, so judgement, handling a difficult customer and knowing when the tool is wrong become what separates workers. The WEF finds that 77% of employers plan to retrain staff, while 63% name skills gaps as their main barrier.

New work being created

The first new sector is physical. AI runs in buildings, and buildings need electricians. McKinsey estimates cumulative global data centre investment could reach $6.7 trillion by 2030, and the electricians’ union IBEW says electrical work is 45 to 70% of a data centre’s construction cost. Microsoft president Brad Smith has named the shortage of electrical talent as the biggest problem slowing its US data centre expansion. Fortune reports projections of more than 300,000 extra electricians needed over the next decade, while about 20,000 union electricians retire each year. Journeymen in one IBEW local near Washington, D.C. earn about $59.50 an hour, over $120,000 a year, and US commercial apprenticeship applications rose from about 70,000 to 120,000 between 2022 and 2024. The irony is hard to miss: the same boom squeezes entry-level desk jobs and creates well-paid work that needs no degree. Unemployment among recent college graduates is 5.6%, the highest in over a decade outside the pandemic.

The second layer is specialist. The WEF expects 170 million new roles by 2030 against 92 million displaced, a net gain of 78 million, with the fastest growth in AI and big data, networks, cybersecurity, renewable energy and environmental engineering. These are survey-based forecasts, and the net figure is global.

The third is oversight, and here I am less sure of the numbers. Someone has to check what a model produced, and take responsibility when it is wrong: in hospitals, law firms, banks, insurers. Titles like AI auditor or model evaluator are appearing, but they are too new to count reliably. History suggests we are bad at naming future jobs: Goldman notes that about 60% of US workers are in occupations that did not exist in 1940, and that more than 85% of employment growth since then came from technology-driven job creation.
