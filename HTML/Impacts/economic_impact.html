Economic Impact

Part 1 of 4: AI in daily life and where the money goes

Most people met artificial intelligence this way: a phone suggested the end of a sentence, a map rerouted around a crash, a streaming app guessed the next film. Nobody called it a revolution, and that is the point. AI is not arriving as one dramatic event. It is seeping into ordinary tools, one feature at a time, and the economic effects are building the same quiet way.

This article asks three practical questions. Where does AI already touch daily life, and what is it doing to the economy? Which jobs are under real pressure, and which are changing rather than disappearing? And what new kinds of work are appearing? The evidence so far is more mixed than either the optimists or the doomsayers admit, so where the data is thin, I say so.

by Changlong Ma

Where it already is

Half of American adults now use an AI chatbot, up from a third in 2024, according to a Pew survey from February 2026. Pew notes the 2024 question was worded slightly differently, so treat the jump as rough. And that number only counts people who type to a chatbot on purpose. It misses the AI nobody opens: the filter that stops a fraudulent card payment, the spam folder, the map that routes around a crash, the app that transcribes a lecture.

For most people the daily effect is small: a few minutes saved on a draft, a translation good enough to read an email in a language you do not speak, a first explanation of a topic that you then check. For a company the same small saving repeats thousands of times a day. That is where economics starts.

Where the money goes

The honest summary is that the money is arriving faster than the proof. The IMF estimates that AI-related investment added about half a percentage point to US GDP growth in 2025, and cites outside estimates that global spending could top $2 trillion in 2026. Much of that is data centres, chips and power, not software people use. The same IMF section warns that the payoff from debt-financed AI investment “could prove illusory”, and that a bust could mean sharp market falls and layoffs.

The benefits are also uneven. IMF research finds that workers with AI-related skills earn more, and that people in low-skill service jobs who serve higher earners benefit too. But cities and regions with more AI-related jobs are not, overall, seeing faster job growth. In the IMF’s survey of US firms, AI use in the information sector is several times higher than in mining or agriculture. So far the boom is concentrated in a few industries and a few kinds of skill.
