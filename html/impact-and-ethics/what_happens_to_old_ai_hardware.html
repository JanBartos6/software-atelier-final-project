What Happens to Old AI Hardware?

Is it discarded or recycled? What steps are taken?

What happens to AI servers when they’re replaced? This article explores how old hardware can get a second life through reuse, repairs, or recycling.

by Leonida Ilijoska

Since the AI Boom and rise of data centers, the demand of GPUs, servers, storage and networking equipment has risen to unprecedented level. Big corporate companies and data center cycle thousands hard drives, network switches and servers in just a few years. The amount of hardware waste this creates makes people question about its environmental repercussions. The solution to the evergrowing demand and saving the environment is simple - just reuse and recycle old AI hardware.

Ways of extending its use

A data center might replace its equipment to train a larger AI model or run training jobs faster. An older GPU may struggle with that work, but it is able to run a smaller model that, for example: sorts customer messages. Using a trained model to answer questions, make predictions, or classify information is called inference.

Another way to extend the hardware's use is through software optimization. There are 3 ways you can do this:

  • Dynamic Paging and Memory Management better, more optimized management of cache and memory
  • Quantization: reduce the size of weight representations
  • Speculative Decoding using older GPUs alongside new GPUs.

How old equipment gets a second job

What if even with extending its use the hardware isn't worth keeping in the data center? Instead of throwing it away as electric waste, the AI hardware can be reused by smaller companies, startups, hobbyists, schools and other institutions who do not need high computing power.

Before a server changes hands, someone needs to test its parts and handle any data left on its drives. Reloop describes this work as part of IT asset disposition, or ITAD. Technicians record the equipment and securely erase drives that can be reused. If a drive needs to be destroyed to protect its data, the rest of the server can still be assessed for reuse.

The recyling process

AI's hardware recyling is a little compared to regular waste recycle. The process is meticulous. The facility where the components are recyled must follow strict guidelines. The reasoning for this is:

  • To educe sensitive data exposure risk
  • Regulatory pressure (laws)
  • To recover scarce resources (gold and other rare earth elements)
  • Financial recovery

The process has 5 stages:

  1. Asset collection
  2. Data destruction
  3. Testing and refurbishment
  4. Material Recovery
  5. Responsible Recycling

What recycling can recover

A server that can no longer be repaired or reused may still contain metals worth recovering, such as copper and gold. Recycling those materials can reduce some of demand for newly mined resources. What a recycler can recover depends on the equipment and the facility processing it.

A reused part goes back into service. A recycled part is broken down so some of its materials can go into a future product. When companies report how much equipment they “reused or recycled” as one figure, it’s hard to tell how much actually went back to work.

When is an upgrade worth it?

Consider two older servers. One runs a small job a few hours a week. Replacing it might save little electricity, so keeping it in service could postpone a purchase. The other runs almost continuously. If a newer machine can do the same work using much less power, the savings can add up faster. The environmental trade-off depends on how much electricity the replacement saves, what it takes to make it, and whether the old equipment still has a useful job to do.

The old server has finished its original job. If another team can use it, the whole machine may go back into service. If only a few parts are worth keeping, they can still help repair other machines and keep more hardware in use.

Conclusion

So, what should happen to old AI hardware? A server that can no longer handle its original workload might still be able to run a smaller model or give another team the computing power it needs. If the whole machine can’t be reused, its working parts can help repair other servers, and responsible recycling can recover materials like copper and gold. Before upgrading, data centers should weigh the electricity a new server could save against the resources needed to make it and the useful life left in the old one.
