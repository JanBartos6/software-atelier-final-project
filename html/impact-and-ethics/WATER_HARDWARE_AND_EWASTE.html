Water, Hardware and Electronic Waste

Artificial Intelligence in Real Life · Page 3 of 4

The resources behind AI, from cooling water to the equipment's end of life.

by Lexie Jowi

3. The resources behind AI

3.1 The water required to cool AI systems

Electricity is not the only resource required to operate artificial intelligence. Water also plays an important role. Powerful computer processors generate heat when performing calculations. If this heat is not controlled, equipment can become less efficient or suffer damage.

To prevent overheating, many data centres use cooling systems. Some of these systems rely on water to remove heat from the facility.

Why does AI need water?
  • Computer processors generate heat.
  • Data centres must maintain suitable operating temperatures.
  • Cooling systems remove excess heat.
  • Some cooling systems consume freshwater.

As the number and size of data centres increase, demand for water can also grow. This concern is especially significant in areas that already experience water shortages. Data centres using large quantities of freshwater may compete with local communities, agriculture and other industries for an essential resource.

The hidden water footprint

There is also an indirect water footprint. Electricity production can require water, depending on the energy source and power-generation technology. The total water footprint of AI includes water used inside data centres and water associated with producing the electricity they consume.

Research published in Patterns in 2025 examined the carbon and water footprints associated with major technology companies' data centres. It demonstrated that environmental impacts can extend beyond the physical boundaries of the facilities themselves, particularly through electricity generation.

3.2 What happens to old AI equipment?

Artificial intelligence depends on physical equipment, including:

  • Computer processors.
  • Graphics processing units (GPUs).
  • Servers.
  • Storage devices.

Manufacturing these components requires raw materials, energy and industrial processes. Electronic equipment uses materials including copper, aluminium and specialised minerals. Extracting and processing them can cause environmental damage through mining, habitat destruction, pollution and high energy consumption.

The problem of electronic waste (e-waste)

As technology develops, older equipment may become outdated and be replaced with newer, more powerful hardware. If discarded equipment is not properly collected, repaired or recycled, it can contribute to environmental pollution.

According to UNEP, AI's environmental footprint must include hardware production and disposal, rather than focusing only on operation. This is part of the wider global electronic-waste challenge, which growing demand for specialised hardware may intensify.

3.3 Looking at the entire life cycle

Energy, water and electronic waste are connected. Consider the life cycle of an AI system:

  1. Production:Raw materials are extracted and processed to manufacture computer equipment.
  2. Operation:Servers require electricity to run AI applications and process information.
  3. Cooling:Cooling systems help prevent equipment from overheating, sometimes using freshwater.
  4. Disposal:Outdated equipment may be discarded, repaired, reused or recycled.

What can be done?

Reducing AI's environmental impact requires action at every stage. Companies can:

  • Improve the energy efficiency of their equipment.
  • Use cooling systems that reduce freshwater consumption.
  • Extend the lifespan of hardware.
  • Ensure outdated components are responsibly recycled.

A sustainable approach considers the entire life cycle of AI technology, from extracting raw materials to disposing of electronic equipment.
