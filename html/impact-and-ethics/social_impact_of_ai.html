AI in Everyday Life: Its Social Impact

How artificial intelligence is changing the way we live, learn and work, and who decides how

Most of us use artificial intelligence every day and barely notice it. This article looks at where AI appears in daily life, what good it does, what problems it causes and what society can do about them.

Written by
Eleonora

Introduction

Most of us use artificial intelligence every day and barely notice it. Your phone unlocks when it sees your face. Spotify suggests a song and you actually like it. Google Maps sends you down another street because there is an accident ahead. None of this feels like science fiction, but all of it is AI, which basically means computer programs that learn from huge amounts of data and then use what they learned to make predictions or decisions.

Because AI has become so normal, people rarely stop to ask what it does to society. That is the question of this text. It looks at where AI appears in daily life, what good it does, what problems it causes, and what we could do about those problems. The main idea is simple: AI is not good or bad by itself. What matters is who builds it, who controls it and how people use it.

1. Where We Meet AI Every Day

On our phones and at home

The phone is probably where most people meet AI most often. Voice assistants like Siri or Alexa answer questions and set timers. The camera improves photos automatically and groups them by person. The keyboard guesses the next word, and apps can translate a sentence in a second. At home, smart speakers and thermostats learn our habits and adjust to them.

A big part of AI is invisible because it works behind the apps we use for fun. Netflix, YouTube, TikTok and Instagram all use recommendation systems. They watch what we click, how long we watch and what we skip, and then they decide what to show us next. That means a lot of what we see online has been picked by a program, not by us.

In transport and cities

Navigation apps predict traffic and calculate the fastest route. Some new cars can keep their lane, brake by themselves or park without help from the driver. Cities and transport companies also use AI, for example to forecast how many passengers will take a train or to control traffic lights so that cars wait less. The goal is a city that is faster and uses less energy.

In banks, shops and companies

If a bank blocks your card because of a strange payment abroad, it was probably an AI system that noticed the unusual pattern. Websites use chatbots to answer simple customer questions. Online shops change their offers depending on what we looked at before. Some companies even use AI to sort job applications or to decide who gets a loan. In these cases the person affected often does not know that a program was involved at all.

In writing and creativity

In the last few years, tools like ChatGPT, Claude or image generators have brought AI to everyone. People use them to write emails, summarise texts, translate, make pictures or get ideas for a project. This is useful, but it also raises questions. If a program writes a text or draws an image, who is the author? And what happens to the value of skills that people spent years learning?

2. The Positive Side

Education

AI can make learning more personal. In a normal class, the teacher has to go at one speed for everybody. A learning app can give an easier exercise to someone who struggles and a harder one to someone who is bored. Language apps can correct pronunciation immediately, and an AI tutor can explain a topic again at midnight without getting tired. This helps especially students whose families cannot pay for private lessons.

The risk is that students stop thinking for themselves. If somebody lets a chatbot do all the homework, they finish faster but learn very little. So the real question for schools is not whether students will use AI, because they already do, but how to teach them to use it as a help and not as a replacement for their own brain.

Health

In medicine, AI programs can look at X-rays or scans and help doctors notice signs of illness earlier. They are also used to speed up research for new medicines. Smartwatches track heart rate and sleep, which makes some people more aware of their health. In places with very few doctors, simple digital tools can at least offer basic advice and screening.

Still, a doctor is more than a machine that gives a diagnosis. Doctors talk to patients, understand the context and take responsibility. AI can make mistakes too, especially for patients who were not well represented in the data it learned from. That is why a human has to check the result.

Accessibility

For people with disabilities, AI can change daily life in a very concrete way. Voice control lets people who cannot use their hands operate a phone or computer. Automatic captions make videos and meetings easier to follow for deaf and hard-of-hearing people. Apps that describe a picture or read a sign aloud help people who are blind. Real-time translation also helps migrants who are still learning a new language. In all these cases the technology gives people more independence.

Work

AI can take over boring and repetitive tasks such as entering data, organising appointments or sorting documents. Then workers have more time for things that need creativity or contact with other people. It can also help small businesses and freelancers, who now have tools that once only big companies could afford.

3. The Problems

Privacy

AI needs data, and the data comes from us: where we go, what we search, what we buy, what we say to our devices. The more a company knows, the better its predictions about us become, and sometimes it can guess things we never told anyone, like our interests, our health or even our political opinion.

Many people accept this because the apps are free and convenient. But often nobody reads the terms and conditions, so we do not really know what we agreed to. On a bigger scale, face recognition in public places could be used to watch citizens all the time. If people feel observed, they may start to behave more carefully and speak less freely.

Bias and unfair decisions

An AI system learns from data made by humans, and humans are not always fair. If a company mostly hired men in the past and a program learns from those old records, it may decide that men are better candidates. A well-known study of commercial face recognition systems found much higher error rates for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018).

What makes this dangerous is that a computer decision looks neutral and objective. It is hard to argue with a result when you do not know how it was calculated. That is why people need the right to ask for an explanation and to have a human look at the decision again.

Fake information

It has become very cheap to produce realistic fake texts, photos, voices and videos. These deepfakes can be used for scams, for example a call with a cloned voice that sounds like a relative asking for money. They can also be used to spread political lies.

Maybe the biggest long-term damage is not that people believe fake things, but that they stop believing anything. If every video could be fake, then real evidence loses its power. So we need to learn to check sources, and platforms should clearly mark content made by AI.

Dependence and social effects

The more we let apps decide for us, the less we practise certain skills. Many people can no longer find their way without GPS, and writing everything with a text generator might weaken our own writing. Using tools is not new, but it becomes a problem when we stop understanding or questioning what the tool does.

There are social effects too. Recommendation algorithms are built to keep us on the app for as long as possible, which can mean too much screen time and a feed full of opinions we already agree with. Some people also talk to AI chatbots as if they were friends. That may help someone who feels lonely for a moment, but it should not replace real contact with other people.

Jobs and inequality

AI will change the job market. Some tasks will disappear, especially routine ones, new jobs will appear and many jobs will simply change. The difficult part is the transition. People who can learn new skills quickly will probably profit, while others risk losing their job or being watched more closely at work.

There is also a gap between those who own the technology and everyone else. Developing advanced AI needs a lot of money, data and computing power, and these are held by a small number of big companies and countries. If only they profit, the difference between rich and poor could grow even more.

Environment

AI feels like something that only exists on a screen, but it runs in data centres that use a lot of electricity and water, and the hardware needs raw materials (Strubell et al., 2019). AI can help save energy in other areas, yet its own footprint is part of the picture and should not be ignored.

4. Responsibility and Rules

If an AI system wrongly refuses someone a loan, who is responsible? The programmer, the bank, or the machine? Most experts agree that the responsibility must stay with people and organisations and never with the algorithm itself.

International organisations have listed some basic principles for trustworthy AI: transparency (people should know when AI is used), fairness, protection of privacy, safety, human control over important decisions, and accountability when something goes wrong (OECD, 2019; UNESCO, 2021).

Lawmakers are starting to turn these ideas into rules. The European Union, for example, adopted the AI Act in 2024. It divides AI systems into risk levels and sets stricter conditions for those used in sensitive areas such as hiring, education or the police (European Parliament & Council, 2024). Making such laws is not easy, because too few rules leave people unprotected while too many rules can slow down useful innovation. And laws alone are not enough. Companies have to test their products, and citizens need enough knowledge to ask critical questions.

5. What Society Can Do

Teach digital skills. Everyone should understand the basics of how AI works and know that it can be wrong. Schools should teach how to check sources and how to use AI responsibly, not just how to code.

Help workers adapt. If jobs change, people need chances to retrain, and the money AI saves should not end up with just a few people.

Include different people. The teams that build AI should be diverse, and people with disabilities, minorities and ordinary users should be asked what they need. Many problems are noticed much earlier that way.

Talk about it openly. Questions like "should the police use face recognition?" are not only for engineers. They concern everyone, so they should be discussed in public.

Keep humans in charge. AI is a tool. It should serve human goals, and the final responsibility belongs to people.

Conclusion

AI is already part of normal life, from the apps on our phones to systems that influence hiring, health care and public services. Its effect on society is therefore mixed. It can improve education and health care, give more independence to people with disabilities and save time at work. At the same time it can reduce privacy, repeat unfair patterns, spread fake information and increase inequality.

None of this is decided in advance. It depends on choices made by governments, companies, schools and each of us as users. If we learn how the technology works, demand transparency and share the benefits fairly, AI can be a real help. If we ignore the risks, it can make existing problems worse. So the real task is not only to build smarter machines but also to make sure society is ready to use them wisely.
