AI in Everyday Life: Its Social Impact

Part 1 of 4: where we meet AI every day

Most of us use artificial intelligence every day and barely notice it. This article looks at where AI appears in daily life, what good it does, what problems it causes and what society can do about them.

by Eleonora

Introduction

Most of us use artificial intelligence every day and barely notice it. Your phone unlocks when it sees your face. Spotify suggests a song and you actually like it. Google Maps sends you down another street because there is an accident ahead. None of this feels like science fiction, but all of it is AI, which basically means computer programs that learn from huge amounts of data and then use what they learned to make predictions or decisions.

Because AI has become so normal, people rarely stop to ask what it does to society. That is the question of this text. It looks at where AI appears in daily life, what good it does, what problems it causes, and what we could do about those problems. The main idea is simple: AI is not good or bad by itself. What matters is who builds it, who controls it and how people use it.

1. Where We Meet AI Every Day

On our phones and at home

The phone is probably where most people meet AI most often. Voice assistants like Siri or Alexa answer questions and set timers. The camera improves photos automatically and groups them by person. The keyboard guesses the next word, and apps can translate a sentence in a second. At home, smart speakers and thermostats learn our habits and adjust to them.

A big part of AI is invisible because it works behind the apps we use for fun. Netflix, YouTube, TikTok and Instagram all use recommendation systems. They watch what we click, how long we watch and what we skip, and then they decide what to show us next. That means a lot of what we see online has been picked by a program, not by us.

In transport and cities

Navigation apps predict traffic and calculate the fastest route. Some new cars can keep their lane, brake by themselves or park without help from the driver. Cities and transport companies also use AI, for example to forecast how many passengers will take a train or to control traffic lights so that cars wait less. The goal is a city that is faster and uses less energy.

In banks, shops and companies

If a bank blocks your card because of a strange payment abroad, it was probably an AI system that noticed the unusual pattern. Websites use chatbots to answer simple customer questions. Online shops change their offers depending on what we looked at before. Some companies even use AI to sort job applications or to decide who gets a loan. In these cases the person affected often does not know that a program was involved at all.

In writing and creativity

In the last few years, tools like ChatGPT, Claude or image generators have brought AI to everyone. People use them to write emails, summarise texts, translate, make pictures or get ideas for a project. This is useful, but it also raises questions. If a program writes a text or draws an image, who is the author? And what happens to the value of skills that people spent years learning?
