New Work

Part 4 of 4: what is being created, and what to do about it

The last page turns to what AI is building: new sectors, new kinds of roles, and a few practical conclusions.

by Changlong Ma

New work being created

The first new sector is physical. AI runs in buildings, and buildings need electricians. McKinsey estimates cumulative global data centre investment could reach $6.7 trillion by 2030, and the electricians’ union IBEW says electrical work is 45 to 70% of a data centre’s construction cost. Microsoft president Brad Smith has named the shortage of electrical talent as the biggest problem slowing its US data centre expansion. Fortune reports projections of more than 300,000 extra electricians needed over the next decade, while about 20,000 union electricians retire each year. Journeymen in one IBEW local near Washington, D.C. earn about $59.50 an hour, over $120,000 a year, and US commercial apprenticeship applications rose from about 70,000 to 120,000 between 2022 and 2024. The irony is hard to miss: the same boom squeezes entry-level desk jobs and creates well-paid work that needs no degree. Unemployment among recent college graduates is 5.6%, the highest in over a decade outside the pandemic.

The second layer is specialist. The World Economic Forum (WEF) expects 170 million new roles by 2030 against 92 million displaced, a net gain of 78 million, with the fastest growth in AI and big data, networks, cybersecurity, renewable energy and environmental engineering. These are survey-based forecasts, and the net figure is global.

The third is oversight, and here I am less sure of the numbers. Someone has to check what a model produced, and take responsibility when it is wrong: in hospitals, law firms, banks, insurers. Titles like AI auditor or model evaluator are appearing, but they are too new to count reliably. History suggests we are bad at naming future jobs: Goldman Sachs Research notes that about 60% of US workers are in occupations that did not exist in 1940, and that more than 85% of employment growth since then came from technology-driven job creation.

What to do with this

Forecasts in this area have a poor record in both directions, so the useful question is not whether AI will take your job but which of your tasks it can do today, and what you will be paid for once it does. Four things follow from the evidence above.

  • Use the tools now. Where the effect was measured, the gains went to the people using them, most of all the less experienced.
  • Be wary of jobs that are mostly one routine task done at a desk. Prefer work that mixes tasks, depends on a person’s trust, or carries responsibility when something goes wrong.
  • Entry level is the weak point. When starting out, look for roles where you learn judgement, not only produce first drafts, because first drafts are what is being automated.
  • Do not ignore the physical economy. Some of the best-paid new openings need skilled hands, not a degree.

The uncomfortable part is that the headline numbers may look fine while the transition is rough for particular groups. The WEF’s net 78 million is a global figure. It does not say that a displaced administrator in one city becomes an AI engineer in the next. That gap, and the International Monetary Fund’s worry that the investment boom itself overshoots, are the real economic questions of the next few years.
