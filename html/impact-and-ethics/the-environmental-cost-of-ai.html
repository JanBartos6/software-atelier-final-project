The environmental cost of Artificial Intelligence

Emissions from training and running models

Artificial intelligence is transforming industries, automating complex workflows, and powering everyday digital tools; from drafting emails and writing software code to accelerating scientific discovery, generative AI has quickly become a standard utility across the global economy. Behind every chatbot reply, generated image, and predictive algorithm, however, lies a massive physical infrastructure.

Understanding the true environmental footprint of artificial intelligence requires looking closely at the energy demands of building these systems, keeping them running at global scale, and manufacturing the hardware that makes them possible [1].

by Andrea Dell'Agnello

Training foundation models

Training a modern large language model (LLM) or multimodal AI system is an extraordinarily energy-intensive process: before an AI model can answer a single question, it must process trillions of words, images, or data points across thousands of specialized processors, such as GPUs and TPUs, running continuously at peak capacity for weeks or months [1].

Hyperscale data centers housing tens of thousands of interconnected chips draw tens of megawatts of continuous power to perform billions of matrix multiplications per second.

High-density server racks generate immense heat, requiring energy-hungry liquid or air-cooling systems (and often millions of gallons of freshwater) to keep processors within safe operating temperatures [3].

For perspective, training a single frontier-scale model can consume dozens of gigawatt-hours of electricity, generating hundreds or even thousands of metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) depending on the regional power grid [1].

Also, the final training run is rarely a one-time event: AI labs run hundreds of smaller experiments, hyperparameter sweeps, and failed training runs before achieving a production-ready model, multiplying total energy consumption [1].

The hidden ongoing cost of inference

While training generates headlines due to its concentrated burst of energy use, inference, the process of running a trained model to generate responses for users, often accounts for the vast majority of an AI system's lifetime emissions.

A single text query to a generative AI assistant requires roughly ten times more electricity than a traditional keyword web search [2].

Generating high-resolution images, complex reasoning chains, or synthetic video demands orders of magnitude more power. Unlike training, which happens once before deployment, inference occurs continuously.

When billions of users interact with AI features integrated into search engines, customer service portals, office software, and mobile devices every single day, those small per-query energy costs multiply rapidly into a massive, uninterrupted global power draw [2].

Embodied carbon and supply chains

Evaluating AI's climate impact solely through operational electricity overlooks a critical piece of the equation: embodied carbon. This refers to the greenhouse gas emissions generated before a server is ever plugged in [4].

Manufacturing cutting-edge AI accelerators involves mining rare earth minerals, refining ultra-pure silicon, and operating semiconductor fabrication plants that require vast amounts of energy and specialized industrial gases.

Furthermore, data center buildings themselves require large volumes of carbon-intensive materials like steel and concrete.

Because AI hardware upgrades occur on rapid two-to-three-year cycles to keep pace with computational demands, embodied emissions remain a persistent factor in the lifecycle of AI infrastructure [4].

Looking ahead

Artificial intelligence holds immense potential to help solve environmental challenges, from optimizing electrical grids and forecasting extreme weather, to discovering new materials for carbon capture and battery storage [2].

But, as the industry matures, measuring energy efficiency alongside accuracy and speed will be essential to ensuring that intelligent software supports a sustainable future.
