AI in Everyday Life: Its Social Impact

Part 3 of 4: the problems AI causes

AI also brings real problems. This part looks at privacy, bias, fake information, dependence, jobs and inequality, and the environmental cost of AI.

by Eleonora

3. The Problems

Privacy

AI needs data, and the data comes from us: where we go, what we search, what we buy, what we say to our devices. The more a company knows, the better its predictions about us become, and sometimes it can guess things we never told anyone, like our interests, our health or even our political opinion.

Many people accept this because the apps are free and convenient. But often nobody reads the terms and conditions, so we do not really know what we agreed to. On a bigger scale, face recognition in public places could be used to watch citizens all the time. If people feel observed, they may start to behave more carefully and speak less freely.

Bias and unfair decisions

An AI system learns from data made by humans, and humans are not always fair. If a company mostly hired men in the past and a program learns from those old records, it may decide that men are better candidates. A well-known study of commercial face recognition systems found much higher error rates for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018).

What makes this dangerous is that a computer decision looks neutral and objective. It is hard to argue with a result when you do not know how it was calculated. That is why people need the right to ask for an explanation and to have a human look at the decision again.

Fake information

It has become very cheap to produce realistic fake texts, photos, voices and videos. These deepfakes can be used for scams, for example a call with a cloned voice that sounds like a relative asking for money. They can also be used to spread political lies.

Maybe the biggest long-term damage is not that people believe fake things, but that they stop believing anything. If every video could be fake, then real evidence loses its power. So we need to learn to check sources, and platforms should clearly mark content made by AI.

Dependence and social effects

The more we let apps decide for us, the less we practise certain skills. Many people can no longer find their way without GPS, and writing everything with a text generator might weaken our own writing. Using tools is not new, but it becomes a problem when we stop understanding or questioning what the tool does.

There are social effects too. Recommendation algorithms are built to keep us on the app for as long as possible, which can mean too much screen time and a feed full of opinions we already agree with. Some people also talk to AI chatbots as if they were friends. That may help someone who feels lonely for a moment, but it should not replace real contact with other people.

Jobs and inequality

AI will change the job market. Some tasks will disappear, especially routine ones, new jobs will appear and many jobs will simply change. The difficult part is the transition. People who can learn new skills quickly will probably profit, while others risk losing their job or being watched more closely at work.

There is also a gap between those who own the technology and everyone else. Developing advanced AI needs a lot of money, data and computing power, and these are held by a small number of big companies and countries. If only they profit, the difference between rich and poor could grow even more.

Environment

AI feels like something that only exists on a screen, but it runs in data centres that use a lot of electricity and water, and the hardware needs raw materials (Strubell et al., 2019). AI can help save energy in other areas, yet its own footprint is part of the picture and should not be ignored.
