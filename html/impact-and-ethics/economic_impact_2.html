Productivity

Part 2 of 4: what the studies show, and who is under pressure

The first page was about money going in. This page is about what comes out: the best evidence on productivity, and the workers who are feeling the change first.

by Changlong Ma

What the studies show

On the worker side, the best evidence is a study of 5,179 customer support agents by Erik Brynjolfsson and co-authors. With an AI assistant suggesting replies, agents resolved about 14% more issues per hour. The gain was 34% for the least experienced agents and minimal for the most experienced. In that case AI worked as an equaliser: it copied the habits of top performers and handed them to newcomers.

Scaled up, Goldman Sachs Research estimates that generative AI could raise labour productivity in developed economies by about 15% once fully adopted. Its baseline has 6 to 7% of US workers displaced (range 3 to 14%) and unemployment up by roughly half a point during the transition. Those are projections from 2025, not measurements. And productivity gains do not automatically become wages. Who keeps them, the firm, the customer or the worker, depends on bargaining power. The IMF’s own research finds that middle-skilled workers in highly exposed jobs are the ones being left out.

Jobs under pressure

The pressure is real, but it is landing in a specific place: the first rung of the ladder. A Stanford study led by Brynjolfsson, using ADP payroll data on millions of US workers through June 2026, finds that employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in the same fields show no comparable gap, and the authors find no sign of broad job losses across the economy. Firms are not firing people so much as hiring fewer juniors. The authors call the results early and descriptive, not proof of cause, and the gap has widened with each update since 2025.

It would be easy to blame this on the tech downturn or on interest rates, and the Stanford authors tested both. The gap persists when they leave out technology firms and computer occupations, and when they control for interest-rate exposure and remote work. It shrinks when they control for education, so the story is not simple. Pay has not fallen: the adjustment shows up as fewer jobs, not lower wages. Goldman Sachs sees a similar pattern, reporting in August 2025 that unemployment among 20- to 30-year-olds in tech-exposed occupations had risen by almost 3 percentage points since the start of the year.

That fits the World Economic Forum’s 2025 employer survey, which lists cashiers and administrative assistants among the fastest-declining roles, now joined by graphic designers, and finds that 41% of employers plan to cut staff where AI can automate tasks. The exposed work is the routine kind: data entry, scripted customer replies, first-draft design, basic code.
