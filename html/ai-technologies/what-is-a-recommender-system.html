What is a recommender system?

Every time you open Netflix, Spotify or TikTok, something has already decided what you will see first.

That “something” is a recommender system. It is probably the AI we meet most often without noticing it, and it is the clearest example of an AI that interacts not with the physical world or with our bodies, but with our minds.

by Minh Quan Tran

Artificial intelligence can be defined as the design of agents that perceive their environment and act to achieve goals. Its everyday applications can be grouped by what they interact with:

  • The world: autonomous vehicles and robots.
  • The human voice: voice assistants and machine translation.
  • The human body: medical imaging and wearable health monitors.
  • The human psyche: systems that model our preferences, our attention and our emotions.

The most prominent example of the last group is the recommender system: software that suggests items likely to interest a particular user, such as films, songs, products or posts.

Why do we need it? Because of information overload. Today’s catalogues contain far more options than anyone could ever look through. Nobody can browse every film on a streaming service or every video uploaded in a day, so a system has to choose for us which few items deserve a place on our screen.

What a recommender learns from

To make that choice, a recommender learns from two kinds of signals:

  • Explicit feedback: things we tell the system on purpose, like ratings, likes or a click on “not interested”.
  • Implicit feedback: things the system infers from our behavior, such as clicks, watch time, skips and searches. This is by far the most important source, because we produce it constantly and without thinking.

TikTok, for example, states that its For You feed is based on user interactions (the videos you like, share or watch to the end), on information about the videos themselves (captions, sounds, hashtags) and, with a lower weight, on device and account settings such as language and country.

a recommender doesn’t need to ask what you like: it watches what you do.

One word of caution before going further: many of the numbers about recommender systems come from the companies that build them. In these articles, figures that companies report about their own products are marked as such, because they cannot be independently verified.
