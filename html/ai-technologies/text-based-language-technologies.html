Text-Based Language Technologies

Text came first, then voice

Why did text come first, everyday examples, how a chatbot understands you

by Daniele Gabriele Gambarota

Why text came first

Text is easy for computers to store and search, and the web contains enormous amounts of it. Every article, forum post, product review, or even every book is a potential example from which a machine can learn. That is why text-based AI developed quickly and now sits behind many of the services we use every day.

Everyday examples

  • Autocomplete, autocorrect

    When your keyboard suggests the next word, it is predicting what is most likely to follow what you have typed so far. This fairly simple idea of predicting the next word is also the core mechanism of large language models. What is often a surprise is that your phone keyboard also continuously edits the hitboxes of each letter based on what you are typing. If the system expects a vowel after “th”, the “e” key becomes a slightly larger target, so that a slightly imprecise tap still lands on the letter you most likely meant. Autocorrect works on the same principle: if you type “teh”, the system knows that “the” is far more likely than any other word, and fixes it before you notice.

  • Machine translation

    Services such as Google Translate convert text from one language to another. Around 2016, translation systems moved from phrase-by-phrase methods to neural networks that read the whole sentence, which made the results much more fluent.

  • Chatbots and AI assistants

    Customer-service chats and general assistants often found nowadays in websites can answer questions, summarise documents, and write or correct text. They are built on large language models trained on vast collections of text.

  • Spam

    As previously mentioned, spam filters and email sorting. Email services classify messages by analysing their content, separating spam and unwanted mail from ordinary mail.

  • Search and summarisation

    Search engines try to understand the intent behind a query, not just match keywords. Summarisation tools shorten long articles into a few lines.

  • Sentiment analysis

    Companies analyse reviews and social-media posts to estimate whether people sound positive, negative or neutral about a product..

    So how does a chatbot “understand” you?

    Well, not the way humans do! It splits your text into small pieces called tokens and, using what it learned from enormous amounts of text, calculates which piece is most likely to come next, again and again, until a full answer appears. The system is simply working with statistical patterns.
