Why is it so hard to stop scrolling?

Most of what we watch online is not something we searched for, but something that was put in front of us.

To understand how strong this influence is, it helps to look at a few numbers first, and then at the psychology that makes a feed so hard to leave.

by Minh Quan Tran

The table below shows how much of what people watch is shaped by recommendations. Note the last column: most figures are reported by the companies themselves, and only the Facebook and Instagram result comes from an independent randomized experiment.

Reported influence of recommender systems
Platform Reported figure Evidence type
Netflix About 80% of hours streamed are influenced by recommendations; 20% come from search Article by company authors (2015)
YouTube More than 70% of watch time is driven by recommendations Statement by a YouTube executive (2018)
Facebook / Instagram Switching from an algorithmic to a chronological feed substantially reduced time spent on the app Randomized experiment (2023)

Why do recommendations have so much power over us? Three psychological mechanisms help explain it.

1. Unpredictable rewards

In 1957, psychologists Charles Ferster and B. F. Skinner published the results of years of experiments with animals in a special cage, known as the “Skinner box”, where pressing a lever or pecking a key delivered food. They found that rewards delivered on an unpredictable schedule produce high and persistent rates of responding, much more than rewards that arrive every time.

A feed works in a similar way: most videos are just fine, but a really rewarding one might appear at any moment, so we keep scrolling to find it. This is why feeds are often compared to slot machines. The comparison is useful, but it remains an analogy, not a proven brain mechanism.

2. No natural stopping points

A newspaper has a last page and a TV episode has its end credits. Infinite scroll and autoplay remove these natural stopping points: there is always another post below and another video about to start. Stopping is no longer the default, it becomes a decision we have to make actively.

3. Personalized nudges

The order of a list, autoplay and labels such as “Top picks for you” act as nudges: design choices that steer our decisions without restricting them. We are still free to choose anything, but most of us pick what is at the top. The concept comes from behavioral economics, where it was described long before modern AI. What changes with AI is that nudges become personalized: each of us is steered with the arrangement that works best on us.

Put together, these mechanisms explain why recommenders are so effective at holding our attention. Is that good or bad for us? The answer, as we will see, is less obvious than it might seem.
