World Models: The Present and the Future

How close are we to training robots inside fully AI-generated worlds?

The idea of training robots in simulated environments is not new. Researchers have used physics simulators for years to develop robotic systems.

What is changing is the introduction of generative AI and world models, which could make simulated environments easier to create, more diverse, and increasingly useful for robotic learning.

But how much of this technology is already possible?

by Giulia

What Can We Do Today?

Today, researchers and developers can already:

  • Generate realistic-looking 3D environments using AI.
  • Simulate physical properties such as gravity, friction, and collisions.
  • Train robots inside virtual environments using reinforcement learning.
  • Generate synthetic data for training AI systems.
  • Test robot software before deploying it on physical hardware.
  • Randomize simulated environments to improve the adaptability of robotic systems.

However, there is an important limitation.

A world that looks realistic does not necessarily behave realistically.

For example, an AI-generated kitchen may look indistinguishable from a real one. However, the visual model may not accurately predict the weight of a glass, how water spills, or what happens when objects collide.

This distinction is particularly important for robotics, where small physical errors can have serious consequences.

NVIDIA: Building Tools for Physical AI

NVIDIA is developing several technologies that support robotics simulation and training.

NVIDIA Isaac Sim

Isaac Sim is a robotics simulation framework built on NVIDIA Omniverse.

It allows developers to create virtual environments, simulate robots, and test their behavior using physically based models.

For example, a developer could reproduce a warehouse, place a virtual robot inside it, and test whether the robot can navigate between shelves while avoiding obstacles.

NVIDIA Isaac Lab

Isaac Lab is a framework designed specifically for robot learning.

Built on Isaac Sim, it supports methods such as reinforcement learning and imitation learning, allowing robots to practice tasks repeatedly in simulated environments.

NVIDIA Cosmos

Cosmos is a platform of world foundation models designed for physical AI applications.

Its models can generate or transform visual representations of physical environments, helping researchers create synthetic training data and explore possible future observations.

These technologies serve different purposes.

Isaac Sim provides physically based simulation, Isaac Lab supports robot learning, and Cosmos contributes generative AI capabilities.

Together, they illustrate how world models and traditional simulators can complement each other.

World Labs: From Virtual Worlds to Robotics

World Labs is another company researching spatial intelligence and generative world models.

Its goal is to develop AI systems that can represent, generate, and reason about three-dimensional environments.

In July 2026, World Labs announced the acquisition of SceniX, a robotics company, to expand its work toward physical environments and robot learning.

The company aims to connect advances in world models with systems capable of interacting with the physical world.

One example of its technology is Atlas, a world model introduced in September 2026. Atlas can process different forms of input, including text, images, video, and 3D information, to generate spatially consistent environments.

World Labs has also developed Marble, a tool for generating explorable 3D worlds.

An interesting demonstration of the connection between world building and robotics comes from work integrating World Labs Marble with NVIDIA Isaac Sim.

In this workflow, an AI-generated environment can be imported into a simulator and equipped with collision geometry and other physical properties, allowing a virtual robot to navigate within it.

This illustrates an important direction for the field: combining AI-generated worlds with physics-based simulation tools.

The Biggest Challenge: The Sim-to-Real Gap

Although simulation offers enormous advantages, robots trained in virtual environments do not always perform as expected in the real world.

This problem is known as the sim-to-real gap.

Imagine a robot that has learned to pick up a cup perfectly in simulation.

In the real world, the cup might be slightly heavier, the surface might be more slippery, or the robot's sensors might produce different readings.

Even small differences can cause the robot to fail.

To address this challenge, researchers use techniques such as domain randomization, improved physics simulation, real-world data collection, and further training on physical hardware.

World models may help by creating more varied experiences and learning aspects of real-world behavior that are difficult to program manually.

However, they also introduce their own challenges, including inaccurate predictions and inconsistencies over time.

What Does the Future Look Like?

One long-term ambition is to develop robotic systems that can learn new skills with less human intervention.

Instead of manually designing every training environment, developers could use generative models to create many different situations automatically.

A robot might practice navigating unfamiliar rooms, manipulating different objects, or responding to unexpected obstacles before encountering them in reality.

Future systems could also combine simulation, predictive world models, and real-world feedback into a continuous learning process.

Nevertheless, completely realistic and reliable AI-generated physical environments remain an open research challenge.

Conclusion

World models represent a promising direction for the development of physical AI.

By learning how environments change and how actions affect the world, these systems could help robots make predictions, plan movements, and learn more efficiently.

Combined with traditional physics simulators and reinforcement learning, generative world models may make robotic training safer, faster, and more adaptable.

The future of robotics is not only about teaching machines to act, but also about helping them understand what may happen before they act.
