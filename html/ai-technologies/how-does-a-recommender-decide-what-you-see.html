How does a recommender decide what you see?

Behind every “Because you watched…” row there is a method for comparing people and items.

There are two basic ideas: recommend things that look like what you already liked, or recommend what people similar to you liked. Large platforms combine both, and add a lot of deep learning on top.

by Minh Quan Tran

The classic approaches are three:

  • Content-based filtering: it recommends items similar to those you liked. If you watched three space documentaries, it suggests a fourth one.
  • Collaborative filtering: it recommends what similar users liked. If people with tastes like yours loved a certain album, you will probably like it too, even if it has nothing in common with what you have heard so far. Amazon made this idea famous with its “customers who bought this item also bought” suggestions.
  • Matrix factorization: it represents every user and every item as a list of numbers, a vector of “hidden factors” learned from the data. Nobody tells the system what these factors mean, but one might end up measuring how much a film is a comedy, another how much it is aimed at children. A user and an item that point in the same direction are a good match.

Large platforms combine these methods with deep learning. YouTube is a good example, because it has published how its system works. It uses two stages:

  1. Candidate generation: a first neural network reduces millions of videos to a few hundred that might interest you.
  2. Ranking: a second network scores those candidates in much more detail and orders the few dozen that are actually shown.

An important detail is what the ranking model tries to predict. YouTube’s model doesn’t predict whether you will click on a video, but how long you are expected to watch it. This choice was made because clicks can be fooled by misleading thumbnails, while watch time is harder to fake. It also shows something that is true of every recommender:

every recommender optimizes a goal that someone has chosen.

Clicks, watch time, purchases, likes: each goal produces a different feed, and none of them is neutral.

The feedback loop

Finally, recommenders work in a feedback loop. You react to what you are shown, and your reactions become new training data for the next round of recommendations. This makes personalization very fast: after a few minutes on a new app, the feed already seems to know you. But the loop also reinforces existing patterns, because the system mostly learns from what it decided to show you in the first place.

A system that learns this quickly what keeps us watching is extremely powerful. But how much of what we watch is really chosen by the algorithm, and why do we find it so hard to put the phone down?
