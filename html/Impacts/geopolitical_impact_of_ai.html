The Geopolitical Impact of Artificial Intelligence

How AI is reshaping global power, security and international relations

This article examines four major dimensions of AI’s geopolitical impact: the technological competition between major powers, its consequences for military and international security, the challenges of global governance, and the possible future of the international order.

Author name

Introduction: AI as a New Source of Geopolitical Power

Artificial intelligence (AI) is no longer simply a technological innovation used to make everyday tasks faster. It is becoming a strategic resource that can influence economic growth, military capabilities, political stability and the international balance of power. From analysing satellite images to developing medicines, managing financial systems and generating information, AI is changing the way governments and societies operate.

This transformation has important geopolitical consequences. Countries that develop advanced AI systems, manufacture the hardware needed to run them and attract highly skilled researchers may gain significant economic and strategic advantages. At the same time, countries that depend on foreign technology risk becoming vulnerable to decisions made outside their borders.

AI is therefore increasingly connected to national security and international competition. Governments are investing in research, computing infrastructure, semiconductor production and education. They are also debating how AI should be regulated and how to prevent its misuse. These choices are not only about technology: they concern sovereignty, influence and the distribution of power in the world.

Unlike traditional resources such as oil or natural gas, AI depends on a complex combination of data, computer chips, energy, software, scientific knowledge and skilled workers. These resources are unevenly distributed. As a result, the development of AI may reinforce the influence of countries that already possess strong technological and economic foundations, while creating new opportunities for others.

The Global AI Race: United States, China and Europe

The competition between the United States and China is one of the clearest examples of AI becoming a geopolitical issue. Both countries consider advanced technology essential to their long-term economic strength and national security. AI is central to this competition because it can improve productivity, support scientific research, strengthen military systems and influence the global technology market.

The United States benefits from a powerful technology sector, major research universities, access to investment capital and companies that develop advanced AI models and computing systems. American firms play an important role in designing software, developing AI services and supplying the chips and cloud infrastructure required by many organisations around the world. This gives the United States considerable influence over the technologies used internationally.

China has also made AI a national priority. Its large domestic market, substantial industrial capacity, expanding research community and government support provide important advantages. AI is being developed for applications ranging from manufacturing and logistics to healthcare, public services and security. China also seeks to reduce its dependence on foreign technology, particularly in strategically important areas such as advanced semiconductors.

The rivalry is not limited to creating the most powerful AI model. It also concerns control over the supply chain. Advanced semiconductors are particularly important because they provide much of the computing power required to train and operate sophisticated AI systems. Their production depends on specialised equipment, materials, intellectual property and highly complex manufacturing processes. Only a relatively small number of countries and companies possess the capabilities needed to produce the most advanced chips.

This concentration creates geopolitical leverage. Export restrictions, trade disputes or disruptions to manufacturing can affect another country’s ability to develop advanced technology. Governments may therefore use trade policy and investment controls to protect their strategic interests. Such measures can slow the transfer of sensitive technologies, but they can also increase tensions and encourage competing technological ecosystems.

Europe occupies a different position in this competition. It has strong scientific institutions, industrial expertise and an important role in the global semiconductor equipment supply chain. However, it has generally struggled to match the scale of American private investment and the size of some Chinese technology programmes. European policymakers have consequently placed considerable emphasis on research, industrial capacity, digital sovereignty and regulation.

The European Union’s approach is distinctive because it seeks to encourage innovation while establishing rules for the use of AI. Its AI Act introduces a risk-based regulatory framework, with stricter requirements for certain high-risk applications and restrictions on some unacceptable uses. This approach may strengthen public trust and establish international standards, although businesses must also manage the costs and complexity of compliance.

The wider lesson is that the AI race is not a simple contest with one winner. Leadership can come from different areas: designing chips, building data centres, developing models, supplying industrial equipment, attracting researchers or setting regulatory standards. Countries that control several parts of this ecosystem are likely to have greater strategic independence.

AI, Military Power and International Security

Artificial intelligence is changing the nature of military power. Armed forces have long used computers and automated systems, but recent advances in machine learning and data analysis create new possibilities. AI can process large quantities of information, identify patterns, support decision-making and help military organisations respond more quickly to complex situations.

One important application is intelligence and surveillance. Military organisations collect information from satellites, drones, radar systems, communications and other sources. AI can help analyse these large datasets, detect unusual activity and identify potential threats. This may allow commanders to obtain a clearer picture of events, although the quality of the result depends on the accuracy of the data and the reliability of the system.

AI can also support logistics and planning. Predicting equipment failures, managing supply chains and allocating resources are essential parts of military operations. Better predictions may reduce costs and improve readiness. In addition, AI-assisted systems can help analyse possible scenarios and provide recommendations. However, military decisions involve uncertainty, ethical judgement and political consequences. Human responsibility remains essential.

Autonomous weapons are among the most controversial applications. These systems can select or engage targets with varying degrees of human involvement. Some technologies already automate parts of defensive or targeting processes, while more advanced autonomous capabilities raise difficult questions about accountability and control.

The central concern is whether a machine should be allowed to make decisions that can directly result in the use of lethal force. If an autonomous system mistakenly identifies a civilian as a threat, responsibility may be difficult to establish. The problem becomes even more serious if several automated systems interact during a rapidly developing conflict. A technical error or misunderstanding could escalate a situation before human decision-makers have time to intervene.

AI is also changing cybersecurity. It can help defenders detect suspicious behaviour, identify vulnerabilities and respond to attacks. At the same time, malicious actors may use AI to automate parts of cyberattacks, create convincing fraudulent messages or search for weaknesses in digital systems. This creates a continuous competition between those seeking to protect networks and those attempting to compromise them.

Another major security challenge is disinformation. Generative AI can produce realistic text, images, audio and video. These tools can be used to create fake political statements, imitate public figures or generate misleading content during elections and international crises. The ability to produce persuasive material quickly and at low cost may make information manipulation easier to scale.

The danger is not limited to convincing people that a particular false story is true. A constant flow of fabricated material can also weaken public confidence in authentic information. Citizens may become less able to distinguish reliable reporting from manipulation, while governments may struggle to establish what happened during a crisis. This can damage democratic institutions and complicate diplomacy.

AI does not automatically make conflict more likely, nor does it guarantee military superiority. Its effects depend on the systems being developed, the people operating them and the political decisions surrounding their use. Nevertheless, countries that integrate AI effectively into defence, intelligence and cybersecurity may gain important advantages. This creates pressure on other states to invest as well, potentially contributing to a new technological arms race.

Economic Power, Digital Dependence and Global Inequality

The geopolitical impact of AI extends far beyond military competition. AI may transform productivity and economic organisation, influencing which countries attract investment, create high-value industries and shape global trade.

Businesses can use AI to automate repetitive tasks, improve forecasts, analyse markets and develop new products. In manufacturing, AI systems can help monitor production and predict equipment failures. In healthcare, they can support medical research and assist professionals in analysing information. In agriculture, they can help monitor crops and manage resources. These applications may increase efficiency and create new economic opportunities.

However, the benefits will not necessarily be distributed equally. Countries with reliable electricity, high-speed networks, advanced research institutions, access to computing power and skilled workers are better positioned to adopt AI. Wealthier economies may therefore benefit earlier and more extensively. Countries with weaker infrastructure could find it difficult to compete, even if their populations have the talent and ambition to participate.

This raises the possibility of a new digital divide. In the past, international inequality was often discussed in terms of access to industrial infrastructure, education and financial resources. In the AI era, access to computing capacity, high-quality data and advanced digital services may become equally important. If a small number of countries control the infrastructure on which others depend, existing economic inequalities could become more difficult to overcome.

Dependence on foreign technology is also a question of sovereignty. Many governments and businesses rely on international providers for cloud computing, software, data storage and AI services. These services can be efficient and affordable, but reliance on a limited number of suppliers creates risks. Changes in prices, access policies, export restrictions or international sanctions may affect a country’s ability to use essential digital infrastructure.

For this reason, governments increasingly discuss technological sovereignty: the ability to make independent decisions about critical technologies and maintain access to essential systems. Complete technological independence is unrealistic for most countries because modern supply chains are international. A more practical objective is to reduce excessive dependence, diversify suppliers, develop local expertise and establish reliable partnerships.

AI may also change the relationship between governments and multinational corporations. Some private companies control enormous computing resources, advanced models and global digital platforms. Their decisions can influence access to information, business opportunities and technological development across national borders. Governments must therefore consider how to regulate powerful companies while preserving innovation and avoiding excessive concentration of power.

For developing countries, AI presents both risks and opportunities. It may help improve education, public administration, agriculture and healthcare without requiring every country to build an entire technology industry from the beginning. Local researchers and entrepreneurs can adapt existing tools to regional languages and needs. Yet these opportunities depend on access, affordability, appropriate training and the ability to protect local data and interests.

The geopolitical question is consequently not only which country develops the most advanced AI. It is also which countries can use AI productively, participate in its development and benefit from the value it creates. International cooperation, education and investment in digital infrastructure will be important in preventing AI from widening the gap between wealthy and poorer societies.

Global Governance and the Future of International Relations

AI develops across national borders, but laws and political institutions remain largely organised at the national level. This creates a major governance challenge. A system developed in one country may be used by businesses and individuals around the world. Its effects can cross borders, while responsibility for its design, deployment and misuse may be spread across several organisations and jurisdictions.

Governments are responding in different ways. Some prioritise innovation and private-sector development. Others place greater emphasis on state supervision, security or social control. The European Union has pursued a regulatory approach based on the level of risk associated with different AI applications. International organisations and diplomatic forums have also encouraged discussions about safety, human rights, transparency and responsible development.

However, reaching international agreement is difficult. Countries do not necessarily share the same political values, economic interests or security priorities. A government may see a technology as an opportunity for economic growth, while another may consider the same technology a security threat. States may also hesitate to accept rules that they believe could weaken their strategic position.

Despite these differences, certain principles could form the basis of cooperation. Governments can work towards greater transparency in high-impact AI systems, clearer responsibility when systems cause harm, stronger cybersecurity practices and safeguards against discrimination. International dialogue may also help establish expectations concerning autonomous weapons and the use of AI during armed conflict.

Cooperation is particularly important because AI-related risks are not restricted to individual countries. Cyberattacks can affect international infrastructure, disinformation can spread across borders, and failures in widely used systems can have consequences for many economies. Scientific cooperation can also help researchers identify technical risks and develop more reliable systems.

At the same time, international governance must avoid creating rules so restrictive that they prevent smaller countries and organisations from participating in technological development. If regulation is designed only by the most powerful states and companies, it may reinforce existing inequalities. Developing countries need a meaningful role in discussions about standards, access, education and the distribution of benefits.

The future geopolitical order will probably be shaped by several competing trends. First, technological competition is likely to continue as countries seek greater control over strategic infrastructure. Second, economic interdependence will remain important because no country can easily produce every component of the AI supply chain independently. Third, international institutions will face increasing pressure to develop rules that keep pace with technological change.

AI could therefore produce two very different outcomes. In one scenario, competition becomes increasingly intense. Countries restrict access to technology, build separate digital ecosystems and treat AI primarily as an instrument of national power. This could deepen mistrust, increase inequality and make international cooperation more difficult.

In another scenario, governments recognise that shared challenges require common solutions. They maintain strategic competition but cooperate on safety standards, scientific research, cybersecurity and the prevention of serious abuses. Such cooperation would not eliminate geopolitical rivalry, but it could reduce the risk that technological competition becomes destabilising.

Conclusion: AI Will Help Define the Next Balance of Power

Artificial intelligence is becoming one of the most important technologies shaping international relations. It influences economic competitiveness, military capabilities, political communication and the independence of states. As a result, the ability to develop and use AI is increasingly connected to national power.

The competition between the United States and China demonstrates how technological leadership has become a strategic priority. Europe’s regulatory approach shows that influence can also come from establishing standards and shaping how technologies are used. Meanwhile, the military applications of AI raise urgent questions about human control, accountability and international security.

Yet the most important issue may be how the benefits and risks of AI are distributed. If access to computing power, expertise and infrastructure remains concentrated in a small number of countries and companies, AI could reinforce existing inequalities. If access becomes broader and international cooperation improves, the technology could also support economic development and help address shared problems.

Ultimately, AI is not simply a tool that countries use; it is a technology that may reshape the relationships between them. The future balance of power will depend not only on who builds the most advanced systems, but also on who controls the infrastructure, establishes the rules and ensures that technological progress serves society. The challenge for governments is to pursue innovation and national security without sacrificing international stability, human rights and cooperation.
