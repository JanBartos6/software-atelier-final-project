What Happens to Old AI Hardware?

The Environmental Cost of Replacing Servers

What happens to an AI server when a faster one takes its place? An upgrade can save electricity, but manufacturing the replacement takes energy and materials, and the old equipment may end up as electronic waste. This article explores that trade-off and looks at how repairing servers or reusing their parts can keep them useful for longer.

by Leonida Ilijoska

A server can still work when a data center decides to replace it. Newer chips may handle more requests or use less electricity for the same task, which can make an upgrade worthwhile. But replacing a server creates two environmental questions. What happens to the old equipment, and what resources go into making its replacement?

When retired hardware becomes waste

Some retired servers can go back into use for less demanding jobs. Others can be taken apart so working components can be used for repairs. When equipment is discarded, it becomes electronic waste, or e-waste. This waste can contain valuable materials such as copper and gold, as well as substances that can pollute the environment if the equipment is handled improperly.

The Global E-waste Monitor 2024 estimated that the world generated 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022. Only 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled. That figure covers discarded electronics of all kinds, including household appliances and computers. It gives a sense of the scale of the broader waste problem.

The amount caused specifically by AI is harder to measure. A 2024 study in Nature Computational Science projected that generative AI hardware, with a focus on large language models, could produce 1.2 to 5.0 million tonnes of e-waste in total from 2020 to 2030. The researchers modeled different rates of AI growth and hardware replacement. The resulting range is a projection, so it cannot tell us how much AI hardware has already been discarded.

Giving old servers a second life

Reusing equipment can be as simple as taking a working drive out of a retired server and installing it in another machine. Microsoft piloted the internal reuse of solid-state drives at one of its facilities. It also reported that it reused or recycled 90.9% of servers and components across its cloud hardware in fiscal year 2024. That percentage combines reuse and recycling across its cloud operations, which include services beyond AI.

Sometimes a newer server’s electricity savings can justify replacing an older one. In other cases, keeping the old server in service longer or upgrading only the parts that need replacing can avoid waste and delay the need for new equipment. The environmental cost of an upgrade depends on how much energy it saves, what it takes to manufacture the replacement, and how much useful life the old hardware still has.
