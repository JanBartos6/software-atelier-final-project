Can a Machine Think?

Why Intelligent Behaviour Is Not the Same as Understanding

In June 2022 a Google engineer called Blake Lemoine told the world that LaMDA, a chatbot he was testing, had become sentient: according to him, it had feelings and was aware of itself. Google answered that his claims were "wholly unfounded", put him on leave and, a month later, fired him for breaking its data security rules. Most AI researchers agreed with Google: LaMDA was a program that had learned to produce very convincing human language. But the question remains interesting. If a machine talks like a person, how do we know it does not think like one?

by Alessio Giugno

Doing Is Not Understanding

The word "intelligence" makes AI sound like a human mind inside a computer. But performing an intelligent task does not necessarily mean thinking or understanding the way a person does. A translation app can translate a joke without finding it funny. A photo app can recognize your grandmother without knowing who she is. An AI system can recognize an object or write a convincing answer without having experiences, emotions or awareness.

Keeping these two things separate helps us describe what AI can do, without giving it qualities that its results do not show.

Turing's Answer: Look at the Behaviour

In 1950 Alan Turing asked "Can machines think?", and then decided that the question was too vague, because nobody can define "thinking" precisely. He replaced it with a game: if a person chatting with a machine cannot tell it apart from a human, we should accept that the machine is intelligent. The History of AI section tells this story in more detail.

Turing's idea is practical: we judge machines by what they do, just like we do with other people. We cannot look inside someone else's head either. But not everyone agreed that behaviour is enough.

The Chinese Room

In 1980 the American philosopher John Searle proposed a famous thought experiment. Imagine a man who does not know a single word of Chinese, closed in a room. Through a slot in the door he receives notes written in Chinese. He has a huge book of instructions, in English, that tells him which Chinese symbols to write back for every symbol he receives. He follows the instructions perfectly, and the people outside receive excellent answers. They are sure there is a Chinese speaker in the room.

But the man does not understand anything. He is only moving symbols around according to rules. Searle said that a computer running a program is in the same situation: it can produce the right answers without understanding what they mean. In his words, manipulating symbols (syntax) is not enough to get meaning (semantics).

Not everyone was convinced. The most common reply is that the man alone does not understand Chinese, but the whole system, the man together with the book and the room, does. More than 40 years later, philosophers are still debating it.

The ELIZA Effect

Humans make this problem even harder, because we are very quick to see a mind where there is none. In 1966 the MIT professor Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA, one of the first chatbots. It worked with simple tricks, like turning the user's sentences into questions. Still, many people who used it felt understood, and some told it very personal things. Weizenbaum was shocked. Ten years later he wrote that he had not realized "that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people".

This tendency now has a name, the ELIZA effect. The writer Douglas Hofstadter described it as "the susceptibility of people to read far more understanding than is warranted into strings of symbols". The chatbots of today are far more complex and convincing than ELIZA, so the effect is even stronger. Blake Lemoine was an expert, and it happened to him too.

Choosing Our Words

We often say that an AI "knows", "thinks", "understands" or "wants" something. These words are useful shortcuts, but they can make us trust a machine more than we should. A better habit is to ask three simple questions: what does the system actually do, how well does it do it, and how was that checked?

Whether a machine could one day truly think is still an open question. To discuss it seriously, we first need the right vocabulary. The next article explains the key terms: machine learning, deep learning and generative AI.
