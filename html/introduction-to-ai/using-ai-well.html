Using AI Well

How to Stay in Control

A man asked the chatbot on Air Canada's website about the discount the airline gives after a death in the family. The chatbot gave him the wrong information, and when he asked for the money the airline refused to pay. In front of the tribunal, Air Canada argued that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The tribunal didn't agree: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website."

by Eros Butti

The Lesson

It seems absurd that a company tried to blame its own chatbot, but the lesson is simple: AI is useful, but it is not always right and it is not always on your side. Here are three things to watch out for.

Warning 1: It Can Be Confidently Wrong

A chatbot rarely says "I don't know". It answers with the same confidence when it is right and when it is wrong, and this is exactly what makes its mistakes hard to see. In 2025, 22 public broadcasters from 18 countries, including the BBC, asked ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity more than 3,000 questions about the news. 45% of the answers had at least one significant problem, and 20% had major errors, such as wrong facts or invented details.

And it doesn't only happen to normal people. In 2023 two lawyers in New York were fined 5,000 dollars because they submitted court cases that ChatGPT had made up. In 2025 the consulting company Deloitte had to give back part of its fee to the Australian government, because a 440,000-dollar report written with the help of AI contained a quote from a judge that was never said and references to reports that don't exist.

Warning 2: What You Type Doesn't Stay With You

When you write something to a chatbot, it doesn't stay on your phone or laptop: it is sent to the company's servers. In 2023 Samsung limited the use of ChatGPT at work after some employees pasted secret company code into it. In 2024 Italy's data protection authority fined OpenAI 15 million euros for training ChatGPT on personal data "without having an adequate legal basis".

Warning 3: Some Apps Want Your Time

Not all the AI around us is something we use on purpose. Some of it is designed to keep us using it, and even the people who built it admit it. Aza Raskin, who invented infinite scroll in 2006, now says that "in technology, incentives eat intentions" and that he had to "write software to break my own addiction". Tristan Harris, a former design ethicist at Google, described it like this: "Every time I check my phone, I'm playing the slot machine to see, 'What did I get?'" Reed Hastings, co-founder of Netflix, was even more direct in 2017: "we're competing with sleep".

TikTok's own research, which came out in 2024, found that 260 videos are enough to form a habit, and since many videos last only a few seconds, that can be just 35 minutes. According to internal messages, its time-limit reminder was mostly meant to improve public trust, not to make people use the app less. In 2025, 45% of American teenagers said they spend too much time on social media, and 45% said it hurts their sleep. In 2026 the European Commission found, in a first decision that is not yet final, that the addictive design of TikTok, Instagram and Facebook breaks EU law.

None of this means that we should stop using AI. It means checking what it tells us, being careful with what we tell it, and noticing when an app is using us more than we are using it.
